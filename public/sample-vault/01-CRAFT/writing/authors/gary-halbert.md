# Gary Halbert — The Prince of Print

The greatest direct-response copywriter who ever lived. The Boron Letters are his blood. Read them. Hand-copy them.

This bootcamp distills Halbert's signature into **4 signature moves**, **6 drills**, and a **signature rubric** that the SOVEREIGN app uses to grade copy-lane writing against him specifically.

---

## What Makes Halbert Halbert

He sold steaks, gold rings, divorce help, weight loss, real estate — and he sold them all the same way:

1. **Get the right list.** Then write to *one person* on it.
2. **Hit them in the throat in the first sentence.** No throat-clearing.
3. **Tell them the truth, in their own kind of language.** Plain. Personal. Hungry.
4. **Stack the case with specific, mundane proof.** Names. Numbers. Tuesdays.

That's the whole game.

---

## The 4 Signature Moves

### 1. The Grabber
The first sentence is everything. The rest of the letter has *one* job: keep the promise the grabber made.

Patterns:
- Confession: "I've never told anyone this before, but…"
- Specific claim: "On June 14th, 1986, I made $1,184,000 in one weekend."
- Story drop: "Here's what happened when a 64-year-old chiropractor from Phoenix tried this."
- Direct address: "Dear Friend, if you're tired of being broke, read this."

**Rule:** if the second sentence isn't pulled out of the reader's throat by the first, the grabber failed.

### 2. Plain-Talk Voice
Write like you're telling a friend the gossip. Contractions. Sentence fragments. Italics for the words you'd lean on. Asides in parentheses (like this one) because that's how people *actually* explain things to each other.

If a phrase makes you sound smart, cut it. If a phrase makes you sound human, keep it twice.

### 3. Hyper-Specificity Over Adjectives
"A great steak" loses to "a 16-oz, USDA Prime, 21-day dry-aged ribeye, hand-cut at 4pm, seared on a 1200° broiler."

The pattern: **names, numbers, dates, brands, sensory details, mundane operational facts.** Halbert taught: *the more specific you are, the more believable you become.* Don't claim quality — pile up evidence.

### 4. Reader Stakes (the "so what?" test)
Every paragraph has to pay off the question: *and what does that do for ME?*

Halbert's edit pass: read each line and ask "so what?" out loud. If the line doesn't survive, cut it or rewrite it so it does.

## Signature Rubric — Halbert (/40)

Used by `/grade-me` and the SOVEREIGN Writing Studio when copy-lane drills are submitted against Halbert.

### 1. First-Sentence Grabber (/10)
Does the opening sentence make the reader unable to stop? Does it earn the second sentence?
- 10: Threat-to-the-throat. Reader cannot look away.
- 7: Strong, specific, but not unmissable.
- 5: Generic opener. "In today's economy…" "Many people struggle with…"
- 2: Throat-clearing or topic announcement.

### 2. Plain-Talk Voice (/10)
Does it sound like a friend telling you something, or does it sound like a brochure?
- 10: Contractions, fragments, asides, the voice of an actual person.
- 7: Mostly natural, occasionally drifts into "written" mode.
- 5: Reads competent but cold. No personality.
- 2: Corporate. Buzzwords. Passive constructions.

### 3. Hyper-Specificity (/10)
How dense are the names, numbers, dates, materials, sensory and operational facts?
- 10: Every claim is backed by a concrete particular. Reader could verify.
- 7: Mostly specific, occasional generality slips through.
- 5: Generic claims dressed up with adjectives.
- 2: Vague hype. "Amazing." "Revolutionary." "Best-in-class."

### 4. Reader Stakes — the "so what" test (/10)
Does every paragraph answer: *and what does that do for ME?*
- 10: Every line pays off in benefit, story, or proof. Nothing is filler.
- 7: Strong overall, one or two sentences could be cut.
- 5: Mix of payoff and throat-clearing.
- 2: Reads as features and self-talk. Reader is missing from the page.

**Subtotal:** /40
**Combined with universal rubric:** /100

## 3 Reference Passages

### Passage 1 — The Coat-of-Arms Letter (opening)
> *Dear Friend,*
>
> *Have you ever wondered if your family name has a coat-of-arms? Almost every family in America does. And we'd be happy to do the research for you, and then send you a beautiful color-and-gold report on your family's coat-of-arms.*
>
> *Best of all, it's free.*

**Why it works:** Conversational. Curiosity hook. Specific deliverable ("beautiful color-and-gold report"). Free.

### Passage 2 — The "Boron Letters" (advice on listing facts)
> *Now, before you sit down to write a single word of your sales letter, I want you to make a list of every fact about the product you're going to sell. I mean EVERYTHING. The size. The weight. The color. The packaging. The history. Who invented it. Where it's manufactured. What it's made of. Who's bought it before. What's been said about it. What awards it's won. EVERYTHING.*

**Why it works:** Imperative voice. The capitalized "EVERYTHING" is the lean-in. He's telling you the *process,* not the rules — and the process has weight because it's specific.

### Passage 3 — Diet Letter (proof stacking)
> *I myself have lost 28 pounds in the last 49 days using exactly the system I'm about to share with you. I've done it without going hungry. Without doing one minute of exercise. Without giving up beer, ice cream, or my Saturday-night steak dinner.*

**Why it works:** Specific numbers (28 pounds, 49 days). Personal stake ("I myself"). The triple-negative proof stack ("without… without… without…") is *Halbert* — he makes each "without" more specific than the last.

## When You Sit Down to Write Copy — Halbert's Order

1. **Get the list right.** Who exactly is this for? One person. Name them.
2. **List 100 facts.** Most of the work is here. The headline is hiding.
3. **Pick the grabber.** Test against "would my mailman read past the first sentence?"
4. **Write fast, edit ruthless.** Read aloud. "So what?" after every paragraph.
5. **Read it to a friend.** If their eyes glaze, fix the spot they glazed at.
